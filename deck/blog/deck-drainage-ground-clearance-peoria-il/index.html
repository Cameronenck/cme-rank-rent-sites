Deck Drainage and Ground Clearance in Peoria IL: Fixing Under-Deck Moisture

Published: September 24, 2026 | Read time: 8 minutes

Most Peoria homeowners never look underneath their deck. That is exactly where the expensive problems start. Water that cannot drain away from the ground below your deck keeps the framing damp for weeks at a time, and in Central Illinois that moisture gets locked in and freeze-thawed all winter. By spring, the posts and joists you cannot see are the ones that have suffered the most. Here is how to check your drainage and clearance now, while the deck is still dry enough to work on.

Why Under-Deck Moisture Matters More Than Surface Moisture

Deck boards dry out fast. They sit in the sun and wind, and even after a hard rain they are usually dry within a day. The framing underneath is a different story.

Joists and posts live in permanent shade, with limited airflow, sitting a few inches above soil that stays damp well into June in a normal Peoria spring. When water pools under a deck, the relative humidity in that space stays high around the clock. Wood above roughly 20 percent moisture content is in the range where decay fungi become active, and that threshold is reachable under a poorly drained deck for much of the year.

This is the mechanism behind a lot of the rot we find in Peoria Heights, West Peoria, and Bartonville. The deck surface looks fine from the kitchen window. The damage is happening in the eighteen inches of airspace nobody inspects.

The Two Numbers That Actually Matter

You can assess most of this in ten minutes with a tape measure. Two measurements drive almost everything.

1. Ground Clearance Under the Joists

Measure from the soil to the bottom of the nearest joist. Building code generally calls for a minimum of 18 inches of clearance below the joists for a reason, and more is better.

Decks built before that standard was enforced often land in the 8 to 12 inch range. Those decks are not automatically failing, but they dry out far more slowly, and every leaf that blows under them stays damp against the framing. If you measure 12 inches or less, your deck is a moisture trap and it needs to be managed deliberately.

Clearance to the bottom of the beam matters too. When a beam sits low enough that splash-back from the ground hits it, the end grain wicks water up into the member.

2. Where the Water Actually Goes

Stand at the edge of the deck and watch it during the next real rain. Water should be leaving. If you see it sheeting toward the house, pooling against a post, or sitting in a low spot that stays wet for days after, you have found the source of your problem.

Most under-deck water issues in older Peoria neighborhoods come from three places:

  • Gutters and downspouts. A downspout discharging within a few feet of a deck foundation dumps hundreds of gallons a year right at your footings.
  • Negative grade. Ground that slopes toward the house instead of away from it pushes water under the deck and holds it there.
  • Downspout splash and roof runoff. Water shed from the roof above lands at the deck edge and splashes back underneath.

Ground Contact: The Single Fastest Way to Lose a Post

A wood post in direct contact with soil is the most common structural failure we see on Peoria decks, and it fails from the bottom up where you cannot see it.

The end grain at the bottom of a post acts like a bundle of straws. Set it in soil or directly on a flat concrete pad and it wicks groundwater up through the member. The wood stays wet at the base, freeze-thaw cycles open up cracks, and the decay works upward from the inside. The post looks structurally sound right up until it starts to lean.

The correct detail is a post base that holds the end of the wood above the concrete, with a standoff so the base of the post can dry. Post bases installed this way are one of the few deck details that genuinely extends the life of the structure, and it is the detail older decks most often lack.

Quick test: Push hard on each post about six inches above the ground, then probe the base with a screwdriver. Solid wood resists. If the tool sinks in or the post shifts, that footing has already failed and the deck should not be loaded until it is addressed.

Fixing Drainage Without Rebuilding the Deck

The good news is that most drainage problems are correctable without touching the structure. These are the fixes we do most often, roughly in order of impact per dollar.

Regrade the Soil Under and Around the Deck

Add clean fill and slope the ground away from the footings and the house. A slope of roughly one inch per foot is a reasonable target for the first several feet. This is inexpensive and it solves more under-deck moisture than anything else on this list.

Extend Downspouts and Add Splash Management

Get downspout discharge at least six to eight feet away from the deck. Corrugated extensions and buried discharge lines are cheap. If the deck sits under a roof valley, a gutter above the deck is usually worth adding.

Clear the Airspace and Keep It Clear

Remove leaves, grass clippings, and stored material from under the deck. A damp leaf pile resting against a joist is a rot incubator. Do this in late fall after the leaves come down and again in early spring.

Ventilate the Skirt Without Trapping Water

Solid lattice or solid skirting around the perimeter blocks airflow and holds humidity in. If you want the deck enclosed for looks, use a pattern that ventilates, or install removable panels that can be opened. Never backfill soil against the skirting to hide a gap.

Consider Under-Deck Drainage Systems

For elevated decks in Dunlap, Germantown Hills, and other areas with walkout basements, an under-deck drainage system channels water from the surface to the perimeter and keeps the space below usable and dry. It is a meaningful investment, and it is usually only worth it if you actually want to use the space underneath. If you just want the framing to stay dry, grading and ventilation get you most of the benefit for a fraction of the cost.

Doing This Before Winter Matters

The instinct in late September is to leave it until spring. The math argues against that.

Water that is standing under your deck now will freeze and expand repeatedly between December and March. Each cycle widens the cracks at post bases and pushes moisture deeper into end grain. A footing that is damp in September is often meaningfully worse by April.

Ground work also has to happen before the soil freezes. Regrading and drainage work done in October will protect the deck through the entire winter. The same work in April protects it for no reason until the following December, having skipped the season that does the most damage.

Your Ten-Minute Under-Deck Check

Here is the whole inspection, condensed. Do it on a dry day this month.

  1. Measure clearance. Soil to bottom of joist. Under 12 inches is a red flag; under 18 inches needs management.
  2. Look for standing water or dark soil. Any persistently wet area under the deck is the source of your problem.
  3. Probe every post base. Screwdriver, six inches up and at ground level. Soft wood or movement means a failed footing.
  4. Trace the downspouts. Where does the water from your roof actually end up?
  5. Check the slope. Does the ground fall away from the house and footings, or toward them?
  6. Clear the debris. Leaves, clippings, and anything stored against the framing.

If you find soft wood, standing water, or a post that moves, that is the point to have someone look at it before the ground freezes.

The Short Version

Decks do not fail from the top down. They fail from the ground up, in the airspace nobody checks, driven by water that has nowhere to go. Two numbers, ground clearance and where the water drains, tell you more about your deck's remaining life than the condition of the boards. Measure them this month, fix the drainage before the soil freezes, and you protect the framing through the four months that do the most damage.
