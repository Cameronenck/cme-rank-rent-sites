Deck Rot Repair in Peoria IL: Finding and Fixing Hidden Wood Damage

Published: September 21, 2026 | Read time: 8 minutes

Rot is the number one reason we replace decks in Peoria, and it almost never gets caught early. The board you can see looks fine. The damage is happening underneath it, in the joist, the rim, or the post base, where water sits after every Central Illinois rain. By the time a board feels soft underfoot, the framing underneath has often been taking on water for years. Here is how to find it now, before the freeze-thaw cycle locks the moisture in for winter.

Why Peoria Decks Rot Faster Than You Expect

Wood rot needs three things: moisture, warmth, and oxygen. Peoria supplies all three for most of the year, and it supplies them in exactly the wrong pattern.

Our climate swings hard. A wet October day at 60 degrees is ideal fungal growth weather. Then the temperature drops below freezing, the water in the wood expands, and it splits the wood fibers apart. When it thaws, more water gets into the new cracks. Repeat that twenty or thirty times between November and March, and you have a deck that ages roughly three years for every one calendar year.

Older Peoria-area homes make this worse. A lot of the housing stock in Peoria Heights, West Peoria, and Bartonville was built before modern deck flashing standards. Those decks were often attached directly to the house rim with no flashing at all, which means water running down the siding goes straight into the ledger board. A rotted ledger is the most serious rot we find because it is what holds the deck to the house.

The Four Places Rot Actually Starts

We inspect a lot of decks across Peoria, Tazewell, and Woodford counties. Rot shows up in the same places over and over.

1. Post Bases and Footings

This is the classic failure point. A wood post set into or directly on concrete wicks groundwater up through the end grain like a straw. The damage is always at the bottom, behind whatever trim or skirt board covers it, so it is invisible until the post starts to lean.

Push hard on a post near the bottom. If it gives even slightly, or if a screwdriver sinks into the wood, the post is compromised. The fix is a post base that holds the wood above the concrete and lets it dry, plus replacing the rotted section.

2. Ledger Boards and Flashing

The ledger is the board bolted to your house that the whole deck hangs from. It sits in the worst possible spot: horizontal, against the siding, in constant shade, and directly in the path of roof runoff.

Look at the top edge where the ledger meets the house. If the siding above it is stained, or if you can see daylight or feel soft wood when you probe with an awl, that is a flashing failure. This is not a cosmetic repair. A rotted ledger is a structural emergency, and it is one of the few deck problems where we tell homeowners to stop using the deck until it is addressed.

3. Joists Under the Decking

Deck boards shed most water, but the gaps between them let a steady trickle through onto the joists below. Joist tops stay damp for days after a rain, and they are never in the sun.

You can check this without pulling boards. From underneath, probe the top edge of each joist with a screwdriver every few feet. Sound wood resists. Rotted wood gives a dull crunch and the tip sinks in.

4. Board Ends and Stair Stringers

End grain absorbs water far faster than the face of a board. That makes the cut ends of your deck boards, and the stringers on your stairs, the first places to go soft. Stair stringers are especially vulnerable because they are low, close to splash-back from the ground, and often shaded.

How to Check Your Deck in Fifteen Minutes

You do not need a contractor to find most of this. Here is the check we run, and you can run it yourself this week.

  1. Walk the surface. Any board that feels spongy, bounces, or sounds hollow when you tap it is suspect.
  2. Probe the ends. Use a screwdriver or an awl on board ends, post bases, and the ledger. Push, do not just touch. Soft wood tells you immediately.
  3. Pull a couple of boards. Pick two or three boards near the house side, where water collects. What you find on the joists underneath tells you the real condition of the deck.
  4. Look for staining and dark streaks. Black or dark brown discoloration on wood is fungal growth. It means moisture has been sitting there, not just passing through.
  5. Check the fasteners. Rusted-through screws and nails are a sign the deck has been wet far longer than the surface suggests. A corroded fastener loses most of its holding power.

If everything checks out, you are in good shape. If you find soft wood in one or two spots, you caught it early. If you find it in several, it is worth having a professional look before winter, because freeze-thaw will accelerate whatever is already started.

Do You Need to Replace the Board or the Whole Deck?

Homeowners ask this constantly, and the honest answer is that it depends almost entirely on what is underneath.

If rot is limited to surface boards and the joists below are solid, you can replace individual boards and stop the problem. That is a real, permanent fix, not a patch.

If rot has reached the joists, ledger, or posts, the question changes. You can sister a joist or replace a post, and that is often worth doing on a deck with good framing. But when more than roughly a third of the framing is compromised, the repair cost starts approaching the cost of a rebuild, and you end up paying to prop up a structure that is going to need replacing in a few years anyway. We covered how that math works in our guide to deck repair vs. replacement.

One rule of thumb: If the rot is cosmetic and surface-level, repair. If it is structural and reaches the framing, get an estimate on both options before you decide. A contractor who only quotes a rebuild without discussing a repair is not giving you the full picture.

Why Repairing Now Beats Waiting Until Spring

The instinct is to wait. It is September, the deck still works, and there are other things to spend money on. Here is the problem with that.

Rotted wood does not dry out. Once the wood fibers have broken down, the material holds water like a sponge. Every freeze-thaw cycle after that pushes the water deeper and widens the damage. A joist that is soft in September will typically be significantly worse in April, and there is a real chance it fails under a snow load in January.

A repair done in fall, while the deck is still dry enough to work on and temperatures are above the minimum for sealants and fasteners, is cheaper than the same repair in spring. It also protects the deck through the worst four months of the year, which is when most of the cumulative damage happens.

Stopping Rot From Coming Back

Fixing the rot without fixing the moisture source means you will be doing this again in five years. The permanent fix is about water management.

First, make sure the deck is properly stained and sealed. A penetrating sealer on a pressure-treated deck slows water absorption significantly. On cedar, it is closer to mandatory. Second, keep the gaps between boards clear so water drains instead of pooling. Third, keep gutters clean and downspouts discharging away from the deck, not under it. Fourth, clear leaves and debris out of the space underneath, because a damp leaf pile against a joist is a rot factory.

If you are dealing with an older deck and want a long-term solution, composite decking sidesteps the rot problem entirely. Our page on composite decks goes through how that material performs specifically in Central Illinois conditions.

When to Call a Professional

Three situations warrant a call rather than a DIY fix.

If the ledger board shows any sign of rot, call immediately and stop using the deck. That is the connection holding your deck to your house. Second, if a post is visibly leaning or the deck has any bounce or sway, the structure needs an assessment. Third, if you find rot in multiple locations, it is worth having someone check the parts you cannot see rather than treating the symptoms one board at a time.

For a single soft board on a solid deck, replacing it yourself is a reasonable Saturday project. Just make sure you look at what is underneath while the board is off, because that is the only free look at your deck's framing you will ever get.

The Short Version

Rot starts where water sits: post bases, the ledger, joist tops, and board ends. Probe those four spots with a screwdriver and you will know more about your deck's condition in fifteen minutes than most homeowners learn in ten years. If you find soft wood, fix it before the ground freezes.
