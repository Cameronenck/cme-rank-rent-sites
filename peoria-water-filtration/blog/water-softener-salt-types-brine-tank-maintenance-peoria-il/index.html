Water Softener Salt Types and Brine Tank Maintenance in Peoria IL

Published: September 25, 2026 | Reading time: 8 minutes

If you own a water softener in Peoria, East Peoria, Pekin, or Morton, you know the routine: buy salt, dump it in, forget about it. That works right up until the tank clogs with a solid mass and the softener quietly stops protecting your plumbing.

Two things cause almost every salt-related failure: buying the wrong grade of salt, and never cleaning the brine tank. Here is what each salt type is, how often the tank needs attention, and what to do when the salt turns into a brick.

The short version of how the system works: hardness minerals bind to resin beads in the tank, and every regeneration cycle pulls salty brine through that resin to flush them out. The softener consumes salt continuously and depends on that salt dissolving. If it cannot dissolve, regeneration fails and your water goes hard again. A softener also does not remove lead, bacteria, or sediment, so for anything beyond hardness, see water softeners versus water filters.

The Four Salt Types Sold in Central Illinois

Any hardware store in Peoria sells four products, usually stacked on the same pallet. They are not interchangeable.

Solar Salt (Coarse Crystals) vs. Evaporated Pellets

Solar salt comes from evaporating sea water in open ponds and produces coarse, irregular crystals. It is the cheapest option by weight and the most water-soluble of the four. Its drawback is physical: the crystals settle tightly, making bridging more likely, and fine crystals pack a grid plate at the bottom of the tank.

Evaporated salt is made by dissolving mined rock salt underground and re-crystallizing it in a controlled facility. The result is a uniform pellet with very low insoluble content, which is why most residential softeners in Peoria run on pellets. Pellets flow easily, resist clumping, and keep the tank cleaner. The trade-off is cost and a slower dissolve rate.

Block Salt and Potassium Chloride

Block salt is compressed into bricks for cabinet-style softeners where a bag will not fit. Blocks dissolve slowly and predictably but are harder to find locally and pricier, so unless your unit specifies them, skip them.

Potassium chloride is the non-sodium alternative, trading sodium for potassium for households restricting sodium intake. It costs several times more than sodium salt and has noticeably lower solubility, especially in a cold garage in an Illinois January.

Purity Grades

Salt bags are labeled with purity percentages. The higher the number, the less insoluble material, and the cleaner your tank stays. Very high purity salt costs more up front and pays that back in reduced cleaning effort. Rock salt is not a softener product.

What Salt Costs in the Peoria Area

Prices move with season and retailer, but the ranking is stable. Solar crystals are the lowest cost per bag. Evaporated pellets carry a modest premium, and you pay it for a cleaner tank and less bridging. Block salt runs highest per pound and only makes sense for cabinet-style units. Potassium chloride costs several times what sodium salt does. Most Peoria households of two to four people, on water in the 15 to 25 grains per gallon range, use roughly one 40 lb bag every four to six weeks.

How to Fill the Brine Tank Correctly

Overfilling is a common cause of bridging, so do not top the tank off whenever you remember. Keep the salt about half full, leaving a foot of open space above it so brine can rise and reach the salt during regeneration. Never pour salt into the brine well, the vertical tube that holds the float; if salt gets in, the float sticks and the softener stops drawing brine. Check the level monthly, and do not mix salt types, since switching from pellets to crystals mid-tank creates a layered mass that dissolves unevenly.

Note on salt creep: Every bag of salt carries a little dust. Over months, that dust and moisture create a hard crust along the tank walls above the water line. If you see white crust climbing the inside wall, the tank is overdue for a clean.

Brine Tank Cleaning: How Often and How

Cleaning the brine tank is the most neglected maintenance item on any softener. The schedule that works for Peoria-area water: check for a salt bridge or mush layer every three to six months, do a full clean-out once a year, and clean immediately any time hard water returns or you notice a sulfur smell from the tank.

The Annual Clean-Out, Step by Step

  1. Unplug the softener or put it in bypass so it does not regenerate mid-clean.
  2. Scoop the remaining salt into a bucket. Anything clean and dry can go back in later.
  3. If the salt hardened into one mass, do not chip at it with metal. Work the edges loose with a plastic scoop. Hitting the tank wall hard can crack it.
  4. Siphon out the standing brine water, then wash the interior with warm water and a little dish soap. A cup of white vinegar helps dissolve wall scale.
  5. Wipe down the brine well and confirm the float moves freely. Rinse thoroughly, refill with water only to the manufacturer's level, add salt to roughly half full, and run one manual regeneration.

Troubleshooting Salt Problems

  • Bridging. The tank looks full but the water stays hard. A crust forms across the top, so water sits on it and the salt below never dissolves. Break it with a plastic tool and run a manual regeneration.
  • Mushing. Salt turns to wet sand or paste from too much water, usually a brine line restriction, a stuck float, or overfilling. Scoop it out, clean the tank, clear the float and line.
  • Salt creep. White crust climbs the walls above the water line from salt dust and humidity. Harmless until it interferes with the float. Clean it during the annual service.
  • Salt disappearing too fast. A bag a week instead of a month means regeneration running too often, hardness set too high, or a leak. Verify the hardness against a water test.

Choosing the Right Salt, and Keeping It on Schedule

Peoria sits on some of the hardest municipal water in Central Illinois. Whether the source is the city supply serving Peoria, East Peoria, and Pekin, or a private well outside Washington, Metamora, or Dunlap, high calcium and magnesium is the baseline problem, and your hardness setting should be based on your water rather than a default from the installer. Hardness is an aesthetic and equipment problem, not a health hazard. Dissolved contaminants in well water are a different category, covered in our guide to common well water problems in the Peoria area.

For most Peoria homes, evaporated pellets are the default, or pellets rather than crystals if the tank has a grid plate. Sodium-restricted households need potassium chloride and should expect to buy it more often for more money. Homes with very high hardness or heavy iron do better on higher purity pellets with a shorter cleaning interval, since salt will not solve iron on its own.

Between filter replacement schedules and softener upkeep, most homeowners juggle more than they can track. The short version: check the salt monthly and keep it about half full, look for bridging or mush every three to six months, and do the full clean-out once a year. Most manufacturers also suggest inspecting the resin bed every five to ten years, since it degrades with iron or chlorine exposure.
