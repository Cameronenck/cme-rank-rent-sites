Lead in Drinking Water in Peoria IL: What Homeowners Need to Know

Published: September 23, 2026 | Reading time: 8 minutes

Lead is the one drinking water contaminant where there is no safe level. Health agencies are clear that any amount is a concern, especially for pregnant women and children under six. If you live in an older home anywhere in the Peoria area, it is worth understanding where lead in water actually comes from, whether your house is at risk, and what removes it.

This guide covers how lead gets into Central Illinois tap water, why treatment plants are rarely the source, how to test, and which filtration systems genuinely remove lead rather than just improve taste.

Lead Does Not Usually Come From the Water Supply

This is the part most homeowners get wrong. Municipal treatment plants in Peoria, East Peoria, Pekin, and the surrounding communities are required to test finished water and report results to the state. When elevated lead shows up in a home's tap sample, the source is almost always between the water main and the glass.

Industry estimates consistently attribute the large majority of household lead exposure through water to building plumbing materials, not the treated supply. That is why two houses on the same street can test completely differently. The variable is the house.

The treatment plant still matters for one reason: corrosion control. Utilities adjust water chemistry so it forms a protective mineral coating inside pipes, which keeps lead from dissolving into the water. When source water chemistry, treatment, or flow patterns change, that protective layer can become less stable. It is a system-wide variable you do not control.

Where Lead Actually Enters Peoria Area Tap Water

Lead Service Lines

The pipe running from the city main to your home is called a service line. Homes built before the mid-1980s may have a partial or full lead service line, and many were never fully replaced. In older Peoria neighborhoods, some of these lines are still in service. You cannot see them from inside the house, which is why utilities now offer or maintain service line inventory programs.

Lead Solder in Pre-1986 Plumbing

Federal law banned lead solder in residential plumbing in 1986. Every home built before that date could have lead solder at copper pipe joints. Those joints are concentrated at every fixture connection, which is exactly where water sits between uses.

Brass Faucets, Valves, and Fittings

Brass and chrome-plated fixtures manufactured before 2014 are allowed to contain small amounts of lead under older standards. Kitchen faucets are the highest-risk fixture because they are the ones people drink and cook from.

Well Systems and Private Plumbing

Rural homes on private wells around Dunlap, Brimfield, Metamora, and Groveland do not have service lines, but they have all the rest, plus their own piping, pressure tanks, and treatment equipment. Well water chemistry also tends to be more acidic or mineral-heavy than treated municipal water, which can make plumbing more aggressive toward metal.

Who Is Most at Risk

  • Children under six. Their bodies absorb lead more readily than adults, and developing nervous systems are the most sensitive to it.
  • Pregnant women. Lead crosses the placenta, and pregnancy also releases lead stored in a mother's bones.
  • Formula-fed infants. Powdered formula mixed with tap water concentrates any lead present, because mixing removes the dilution effect.
  • Anyone in a pre-1986 home. Age of the building is the single strongest predictor of elevated tap lead.

Blood lead testing is a medical question, not a water one. The water question is simply whether lead is present above the level you are comfortable with, and the only way to answer it is to test the water.

How to Test for Lead in Your Water

Standard water hardness test strips and general-purpose home test kits will not tell you anything about lead. It requires a specific analysis, and the sampling method matters as much as the lab.

  1. Use a certified laboratory. A state-certified lab using EPA methods gives you a defensible number. Peoria-area homeowners can also use the city's water quality reporting and service line inventory information to check whether their line is known to be lead.
  2. Take a first-draw sample. Collect the first water out of the tap after the water has sat in the pipes for at least six hours, ideally overnight. This is where lead concentrates.
  3. Test the kitchen tap. That is where drinking and cooking water comes from, so that is the sample that matters.
  4. Sample before and after your filter. If you already have a filtration system, test both sides. It tells you whether the system is actually doing the job.
  5. Retest after any plumbing work. Replacing a water heater, main valve, or a section of pipe can disturb protective scale and temporarily raise lead levels.
Important: Do not run the tap for ten minutes before sampling. A flushed sample will read low and tell you nothing useful about what your family actually drinks. First-draw is the standard for a reason.

Not Every Filter Removes Lead

This is where homeowners waste money. The filter aisle is full of products labeled for "cleaner, better tasting water," and most of them do nothing about lead.

What Does Not Work

  • Basic sediment filters. These trap particles. Lead is dissolved in the water, so it passes straight through.
  • Granular carbon and pitcher filters (without certification). Carbon improves taste and odor and removes chlorine and some organics. It is not designed to remove dissolved metals unless the unit specifically carries a lead reduction certification.
  • Water softeners. A softener exchanges calcium and magnesium for sodium. It does not remove lead, and in some water chemistries it can make the water slightly more aggressive toward metal plumbing downstream.
  • Salt-free conditioners. These change how minerals behave in water. They do not remove anything, and lead is not on the list of what they address.

What Actually Works

  • Reverse osmosis. RO forces water through a membrane that rejects dissolved lead along with most other dissolved contaminants. For drinking water specifically, this is the most thorough point-of-use solution. See our breakdown of whole-house filtration versus reverse osmosis.
  • Certified carbon block filters. Dense carbon block cartridges rated for lead reduction use adsorption to capture dissolved lead. Certification matters here. A cartridge without a lead reduction rating is not the same product.
  • Whole-house filtration with lead-rated media. A properly configured whole-house system protects every tap in the house, which matters if lead is coming from a shared service line or interior plumbing rather than a single fixture.

If your test shows lead at the kitchen tap, a point-of-use RO or certified carbon block system at that tap is the fastest, lowest-cost fix. If multiple taps across the house show lead, the problem is in the plumbing or service line and a whole-house approach makes more sense.

Reducing Lead Exposure Right Now

While you arrange testing and treatment, there are no-cost habits that meaningfully cut exposure in a home with lead plumbing:

  • Flush the tap before use. If water has sat for more than six hours, run the cold tap for one to two minutes before drinking or cooking. Cold water is the rule. Hot water dissolves more lead and should never be used for drinking, cooking, or mixing formula.
  • Never mix baby formula with unfiltered tap water in a home with known or suspected lead plumbing.
  • Clean the faucet aerator. Sediment and debris trapped in the aerator can hold lead particles.
  • Replace old kitchen faucets. A post-2014 lead-free-rated faucet at the kitchen sink is a cheap, permanent improvement.
  • Do not rely on boiling. Boiling water does not remove lead. It concentrates it by evaporating water away.

How This Fits With Your Other Water Problems

Most Peoria homes are not dealing with lead alone. The same water that can carry lead through old plumbing also runs 15 to 25 grains per gallon of hardness, which is why softeners are so common here. A Reasonable Plan for a Peoria Home

  1. Check your home's build date. Anything before 1986 gets treated as a lead-risk property until testing says otherwise.
  2. Look up your service line material through the city or your utility's inventory program.
  3. Take a first-draw kitchen tap sample and send it to a certified lab.
  4. Address the fixture that people actually drink from first, with certified lead-reduction filtration or RO.
  5. If lead appears at multiple taps, treat the whole house.
  6. Retest after any plumbing change and once a year afterward.

Bottom Line
