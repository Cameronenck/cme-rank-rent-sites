Spray Foam vs Blown-In Insulation: What Peoria IL Homeowners Should Choose

Published: September 2026 | Reading time: 8 min | Peoria, Illinois

If you own a home in Peoria, Pekin, or anywhere across Central Illinois, you have probably heard two names come up when it is time to fix a cold room, a drafty attic, or an energy bill that keeps climbing: spray foam and blown-in insulation. Both are legitimate, code-compliant ways to insulate a home, but they are not interchangeable. They seal differently, cost differently, and perform differently across the four-season climate of the Peoria area. This guide breaks down the real differences so you can pick the right material for your house and your budget.

How Spray Foam Insulation Works

Spray foam is a two-part chemical mixed at the spray gun and applied as a liquid. It expands on contact to fill gaps, cracks, and irregular cavities, then cures into a solid layer. Because it expands into place, it does something fiber-based insulation cannot do on its own: it air-seals the same cavity it insulates.

Open-cell foam is lighter, expands more, and offers an R-value of roughly 3.5 to 3.9 per inch. Closed-cell foam is denser, adds structural rigidity, acts as a vapor barrier, and delivers about 6.5 to 7.0 per inch. Both are applied by a trained installer, which is why spray foam is a professional-grade upgrade rather than a weekend project.

How Blown-In Insulation Works

Blown-in insulation is loose material, typically fiberglass or cellulose, that a machine blows into an open cavity or attic floor to a specified depth. It is fast to install, fills around obstructions better than batts, and is one of the most affordable ways to bring an under-insulated Peoria attic up to code.

Cellulose is made largely from recycled paper and treated for fire resistance, with an R-value of about 3.2 to 3.8 per inch. Blown-in fiberglass sits lower at roughly 2.2 to 2.7 per inch, meaning it takes more depth to hit the same R-value. Either way, blown-in material settles over time.

Air Sealing: The Biggest Real-World Difference

Here is the distinction that matters most in a Peoria home. Insulation slows heat transfer; air sealing stops air from moving. Blown-in insulation slows heat, but it does not seal the gaps around plumbing penetrations, top plates, and recessed lights where warm air escapes in winter and humid air enters in summer.

Spray foam does both jobs in a single pass. In Central Illinois, where winter wind and summer humidity both work against an under-sealed house, that combined effect is why spray foam often outperforms its R-value on paper. A drafty attic insulated to R-49 with loose fill can still underperform a well-sealed attic insulated with foam, because the air leakage was never addressed.

R-Value and Illinois Code Requirements

Illinois sits in a climate zone that calls for substantial insulation. Typical targets for the Peoria area are R-49 to R-60 in attics and roughly R-13 to R-20 in walls. The table below shows how much depth each material needs to reach R-49.

Material R-Value Per Inch Depth for R-49
Blown-in fiberglass 2.2 - 2.7 18 - 22 inches
Blown-in cellulose 3.2 - 3.8 13 - 15 inches
Open-cell spray foam 3.5 - 3.9 13 - 14 inches
Closed-cell spray foam 6.5 - 7.0 7 - 8 inches

The takeaway: closed-cell foam reaches a high R-value in far less space, which matters in a wall cavity or a low-clearance crawl space where you simply cannot fit two feet of loose fill.

Air-seal the critical leakage points, attic penetrations, and rim joists with spray foam, then blow in cellulose across the open attic floor to reach your target R-value at a lower total cost than foaming the entire attic.

Where Blown-In Insulation Makes Sense

  • Open attic floors: If you have accessible, unobstructed joist bays, blown-in cellulose is a fast, affordable way to reach R-49 or higher.
  • Budget-driven projects: When upfront cost is the deciding factor and the home is reasonably air-sealed, blown-in delivers solid performance per dollar.
  • Adding depth to existing insulation: Topping off an under-insulated attic is quicker and cheaper with loose fill than with foam.
  • Large, simple spaces: Wide-open attics with few obstructions play to blown-in strengths.

Where Spray Foam Wins

  • Rim joists and band boards: A major air-leak path that loose fill cannot address. See our band board insulation guide.
  • Crawl spaces: Closed-cell foam seals and insulates in one step in tight, damp spaces. See our crawl space insulation page.
  • Walls and low-clearance cavities: Where depth is limited, closed-cell foam hits high R-values in minimal thickness. Learn more in our wall insulation overview.
  • Attic roof decks: Foaming the underside of the roof deck creates a conditioned attic and brings HVAC equipment inside the thermal envelope. See our attic insulation guide.

Performance Over Time in Central Illinois

Blown-in insulation settles and can lose R-value as it compacts, and it is vulnerable to air movement, moisture, and rodent disturbance. Spray foam cures rigid and holds its R-value and its seal without settling. That durability matters in a climate that swings from single-digit winter wind chills to humid summer days, delivering more consistent energy savings over a 20-year horizon.

Making the Decision in Peoria, Pekin, and Morton

  1. Find the actual problem: Is the room cold because of missing insulation, or air leakage? A blower-door assessment answers this.
  2. Air-seal first: Regardless of the insulation you choose, sealing penetrations and rim joists is the highest-return step.
  3. Match material to space: Foam for tight, damp, or structurally important cavities; blown-in for large, open, dry attic floors.
  4. Consider a hybrid: Seal with foam, blanket with loose fill, and capture the benefits of both.
  5. Compare written quotes: Each estimate should state material, depth, and target R-value so the comparison is fair.

The Bottom Line

Blown-in insulation is cheaper and faster. Spray foam seals, lasts, and performs better per inch. In the mixed housing stock of Peoria, East Peoria, Pekin, Morton, and Washington, the best results usually come from using both where each makes sense.

Common Questions About Spray Foam and Blown-In Insulation

Is spray foam better than blown-in insulation?

Not universally. Spray foam air-seals and holds its R-value better, especially in tight or damp cavities. Blown-in is more cost-effective for large, open, dry attic floors. Many Peoria homes get the best result from combining the two.

Does blown-in insulation need air sealing too?

Yes. Blown-in material does not seal air leaks on its own. Without air sealing at penetrations and rim joists, a blown-in attic can still lose significant heat through air movement. See our air sealing cost guide.

Do I need R-49 or R-60 in my Peoria attic?

Illinois climate targets generally call for R-49 to R-60 in attics and roughly R-13 to R-20 in walls. See our R-value requirements guide for details.

Conclusion

Spray foam and blown-in insulation are both proven tools. The question is not which one is better in the abstract, but which one belongs in each part of your Peoria IL home. Seal the leak paths with foam, insulate the wide-open spaces with loose fill, and you get the strongest combination of comfort, code compliance, and long-term energy savings.
