Furnace Will Not Turn On?

Ignitor, flame sensor, and pressure switch faults explained, plus what you can safely check before calling a technician.

Home / Blog / Furnace Will Not Turn On?
Published: September 24, 2026

It happens on the first genuinely cold morning of the year. You turn the thermostat up, the house stays quiet, and somewhere in the basement or utility closet a furnace that worked fine last spring is now doing nothing at all. Across Ohio, the first cold snap in October and November generates more no-heat service calls than any other week of the year. Homeowners in Columbus, Cleveland, Cincinnati, Toledo, and Akron all discover the same thing at the same time, usually at 6am.

Most furnace no-start problems fall into a small number of predictable categories. Some you can diagnose and fix yourself in ten minutes. Others involve the ignition and safety circuit, where a licensed technician with a meter and a fault code is the only sensible path. Knowing the difference saves you both money and a cold night.

Start With the Simple Things First

Before assuming the worst, rule out the mundane causes. A surprising share of no-heat calls end with the technician flipping a switch or replacing two AA batteries. Work through this list before you call anyone.

  • Thermostat set correctly. Confirm it is in Heat mode, not Cool or Auto, and set the target temperature four to five degrees above current room temperature. A thermostat set one degree higher may simply wait.
  • Thermostat batteries. A blank or flickering display usually means dead batteries. Replace them and give the system five minutes to respond.
  • Furnace power switch. Most furnaces have a light switch style power switch near the unit, often on the side of the cabinet. It looks like a light switch because it is one, and it gets flipped off during summer maintenance and never flipped back.
  • Breaker. Check the panel for a tripped breaker on the furnace circuit. Reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
  • Door panel. Furnace doors have a safety interlock switch. A panel that is not fully seated will prevent the unit from running at all, and it is easy to knock loose while changing a filter.
  • Gas supply. Confirm the gas shutoff valve at the furnace is open and parallel to the pipe. If other gas appliances in the home also fail to light, call your gas utility.

If all of that checks out and the furnace still does not fire, the problem is in the ignition or safety circuit. That is where the following three components live.

The Three Components Behind Most No-Heat Calls

Modern furnaces run a fixed startup sequence: the thermostat calls for heat, the inducer motor starts, the pressure switch confirms draft, the ignitor glows, the gas valve opens, the flame sensor proves ignition, and only then does the blower come on. A failure at any step stops the sequence and the control board locks out after three attempts. The three components that fail most often are the ignitor, the flame sensor, and the pressure switch.

1. The Hot Surface Ignitor

The ignitor is a small ceramic element that glows orange-hot to light the burners, much like the element in a toaster. It is one of the most common failure points in any gas furnace because ceramic is brittle. It cracks from age, from thermal cycling, or from a stray touch during a filter change. When the ignitor fails, the furnace goes through its startup sequence, the inducer runs, and then nothing lights.

You can often see the failure with your own eyes. With the power off and the door removed, inspect the ignitor for a visible crack or a white powdery scorch mark. Do not touch the element with your fingers, since skin oils cause it to fail prematurely. Ignitors are inexpensive parts, typically 30 to 90 dollars, and a full repair including the service call usually lands between 150 and 350 dollars in Ohio. If the furnace is otherwise healthy, this is a fast, straightforward fix.

2. The Flame Sensor

The flame sensor is a thin metal rod positioned in front of the burner flame. It generates a tiny electrical current when flame touches it, and that signal tells the control board that ignition succeeded and it is safe to keep the gas valve open. Over a heating season, the rod accumulates a thin layer of carbon and oxidation. Once that coating insulates the rod, the board stops sensing flame, shuts the gas valve after a few seconds, and locks out. The furnace lights briefly and then quits, sometimes repeating the cycle three times.

Cleaning the flame sensor is part of every proper fall tune-up. A technician removes it, wipes it with fine steel wool or a crisp dollar bill, and reinstalls it. Sandpaper should not be used because it scratches the rod and makes the buildup return faster. Because access varies widely between furnace models and the sensor sits inside the combustion path, this is usually best handled during a scheduled HVAC maintenance visit rather than as a homeowner project.

3. The Pressure Switch

The pressure switch is a safety device, and it is the one you should never try to bypass. It confirms that the inducer motor is pulling exhaust gases out of the heat exchanger and up the flue before the furnace is allowed to open the gas valve. If the switch does not close, the furnace will not fire, period.

Pressure switch faults almost always trace back to one of three causes: a blocked or obstructed flue, a clogged condensate drain line on a high-efficiency furnace, or a failed inducer motor. On condensing furnaces, which are common in newer Ohio homes, the condensate trap and drain are frequent culprits. A blockage causes the switch to stay open and the furnace to lock out immediately, often with no other symptom.

This is the component where a homeowner should stop and call a professional. A blocked flue can force carbon monoxide back into the living space, which is exactly why the safety circuit exists. Our guide on carbon monoxide safety for Ohio homes covers the warning signs in detail. If you smell gas or your CO alarm sounds, leave the house and call for emergency service immediately.

Reading the Fault Code

Nearly every furnace built in the last twenty years has a control board with a small LED window that blinks a fault code. The pattern matters. A single blink repeating usually indicates a lockout after failed ignition. Two blinks often points to a pressure switch fault. Three blinks frequently signals a pressure switch that is stuck closed. The exact code chart is printed on the inside of the blower door on most models, and photographing it before you call saves a technician a step.

Being able to say, accurately, that your furnace is blinking three times and locking out is worth more than a general complaint that it is broken. Technicians can often load the right part before arriving. It shortens the visit and sometimes eliminates the second trip entirely.

Repair or Replace?

If your furnace is under twelve years old and the failure is one of the three components above, repair is almost always the right decision. These are individual parts, not systemic failures, and a repaired furnace often runs another decade. If the unit is fifteen years or older, has a cracked heat exchanger, or has required multiple repairs over the past two heating seasons, replacement usually wins on total cost of ownership. Frequently Asked Questions

Why will not my furnace turn on when I raise the thermostat?

Check the simple causes first: thermostat in Heat mode and set several degrees above room temperature, fresh thermostat batteries, the power switch at the furnace turned on, the breaker not tripped, and the door panel fully seated. If the blower runs but no heat comes out, the issue is in the ignition or safety circuit.

What does it mean if the furnace tries to light three times and quits?

That is a safety lockout after a failed ignition sequence. It points to a failed ignitor, a dirty flame sensor that cannot prove flame, or a pressure switch that cannot confirm draft. The control board locks out after three attempts and will retry only after a reset.

How much does it cost to replace a furnace ignitor in Ohio?

The part typically runs 30 to 90 dollars, with total repair cost including the service call usually between 150 and 350 dollars. It is one of the most common no-heat repairs and is often completed in a single visit.

Can I clean the flame sensor myself?

It is possible but requires shutting off power and careful handling. Wipe the rod with fine steel wool or a dollar bill, never sandpaper, and avoid touching it with bare fingers. Many homeowners have it cleaned during a fall tune-up instead.

What is a pressure switch and why does it stop the furnace?

It is a safety device that confirms exhaust is drafting out of the heat exchanger before gas is allowed to flow. A blocked flue, a clogged condensate drain, or a failed inducer motor keeps it open and stops the furnace. Never bypass it.

Why does my furnace run but only blow cold air?

Usually a dirty filter restricting airflow and tripping the high-limit switch, a failed ignitor so no flame establishes, or a gas supply issue. Replace the filter first, and if the air stays cold, schedule a diagnostic.

Should I keep resetting the furnace when it locks out?

Reset once to clear a lockout. If it locks out again, stop and call a technician. The lockout exists to prevent unsafe operation, and repeated resets on a blocked flue or failing gas valve create a real hazard.

How do I know if my furnace problem is an emergency?

Treat it as an emergency if you smell gas, the carbon monoxide alarm sounds, you see soot or scorching, or a cracked heat exchanger is suspected. Leave the home and call for emergency service or your gas utility. A furnace that simply will not start on a mild September day can wait for a normal appointment.

When should I repair versus replace a furnace that will not start?

Under twelve years old with a single failed component, repair. Fifteen years or older, cracked heat exchanger, or repeated repairs in the last two seasons, replacement is usually cheaper over the next five years.

Does a fall tune-up prevent no-heat calls?

It prevents most of them. A tune-up cleans the flame sensor, tests the ignitor, verifies the pressure switch and inducer, inspects the flue, and confirms the heat exchanger is sound. Most Ohio no-heat calls trace back to something a September tune-up would have caught.
