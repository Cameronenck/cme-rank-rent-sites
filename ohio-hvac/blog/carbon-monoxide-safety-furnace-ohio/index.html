Carbon Monoxide Safety for Ohio Homes

Furnace warning signs, detector placement, and prevention steps every Ohio homeowner should take before heating season.

Home / Blog / Carbon Monoxide Safety for Ohio Homes
Published: September 14, 2026

Every fall, Ohio homeowners fire up furnaces that have sat idle for months. That first cold snap is when carbon monoxide problems show up, and they rarely announce themselves. Carbon monoxide is colorless, odorless, and tasteless, which is exactly why it is dangerous. Understanding how your furnace produces it, where it can leak, and how to catch it early is one of the most important safety tasks of the heating season for families in Columbus, Cleveland, Cincinnati, Toledo, Akron, and everywhere in between.

This guide covers how carbon monoxide forms, the warning signs that a furnace is misbehaving, where to place detectors, and the prevention steps that actually work.

Why Ohio Homes Are at Risk Every Heating Season

Ohio's climate means furnaces run hard from October through March, often for months at a time without a break. The longer a furnace runs, the more wear accumulates on the parts that keep combustion safe: the heat exchanger, the burners, and the flue that carries exhaust outside. A furnace that ran fine last winter can develop a cracked heat exchanger or a blocked chimney over the summer.

The Midwest's older housing stock matters too. In Cleveland, Toledo, and Akron, a large share of homes have furnaces and water heaters that are 15 to 25 years old. Aging equipment combined with tightly sealed, energy-efficient homes can trap combustion byproducts indoors. Add an attached garage with a running vehicle and you have several potential sources of carbon monoxide in a single property.

None of this means your home is unsafe. It means carbon monoxide deserves a deliberate check every fall rather than an assumption that everything is fine.

How Carbon Monoxide Forms in a Furnace

A gas furnace burns natural gas to create heat. When combustion is complete, the exhaust is mostly carbon dioxide and water vapor. When combustion is incomplete, carbon monoxide is produced instead. Incomplete combustion happens when there is not enough oxygen reaching the flame, when the burners are dirty or misaligned, or when the heat exchanger is compromised.

The furnace's flue and heat exchanger are designed to keep exhaust separate from the air you breathe. If either fails, carbon monoxide can mix into your home's air supply. That is the failure mode that matters most, and it is almost impossible to detect without instruments or a professional inspection.

Warning Signs Your Furnace May Be Producing Carbon Monoxide

You cannot see or smell carbon monoxide, but the furnace itself often signals a problem before a detector sounds. Watch for these indicators:

  • Yellow or orange burner flame. A healthy natural gas flame is crisp blue. A yellow, flickering, or lazy flame usually means incomplete combustion.
  • Soot or black streaks around the furnace, vents, or flue. Soot is a byproduct of poor combustion.
  • Excessive moisture on windows and walls near the furnace, or rust flaking on the flue pipe.
  • A backdraft or burning smell when the furnace starts, which can indicate the flue is not pulling exhaust outside.
  • Rust or corrosion on the heat exchanger or flue connections.
  • Frequent headaches or flu-like symptoms among household members that fade when they leave home.

Any one of these is a reason to call a licensed technician. If you also feel unwell, treat it as a carbon monoxide event and get everyone outside immediately.

Recognizing Carbon Monoxide Exposure Symptoms

Exposure symptoms escalate with concentration and time. At low levels, carbon monoxide causes headache, fatigue, dizziness, and shortness of breath. At higher concentrations, it brings nausea, confusion, chest pain, and loss of coordination. At very high levels it is fatal.

The distinguishing clue is context. Flu symptoms spread person to person and last days. Carbon monoxide symptoms hit everyone in the home at roughly the same time, often appear during heating season, and improve within minutes or hours of leaving the house. If your family feels better at the grocery store than in the living room, take that pattern seriously.

What to Do If You Suspect Exposure

Get everyone, including pets, outside into fresh air. Call 911 or your local fire department. Do not re-enter to open windows or shut off the furnace. Once emergency responders confirm the home is safe, have a licensed HVAC technician diagnose the source before you use the heating system again. Never restart a furnace after a suspected carbon monoxide event without an inspection.

Where to Place Carbon Monoxide Detectors

A working detector is your primary defense. Place at least one carbon monoxide alarm on every level of the home, installing the unit outside sleeping areas so it can wake occupants. Keep detectors at least 15 feet from furnaces, water heaters, and other fuel-burning appliances to avoid nuisance alarms, and at least 10 feet from humid bathrooms where moisture can damage the sensor.

Ohio code requires carbon monoxide alarms in homes that contain fuel-burning appliances or attached garages. If your home has a gas furnace, a gas water heater, or a wood-burning fireplace, an alarm is not optional. Check the batteries monthly and test each unit before the heating season starts.

Combination Smoke and CO Alarms

Combination units simplify installation, but keep in mind that smoke and carbon monoxide behave differently. Smoke rises, while carbon monoxide mixes evenly through the air. Some combination alarms mount only on the ceiling, which can delay CO detection. Follow the manufacturer's placement instructions exactly, and consider standalone CO alarms for levels where the ceiling is not ideal.

Choosing and Maintaining a CO Detector

Buy a UL-listed carbon monoxide alarm. Look for a digital display, which shows current CO levels in parts per million rather than just sounding an alarm. A digital readout helps you distinguish a nuisance alarm from a real rise in levels.

Detectors do not last forever. The electrochemical sensor inside degrades over years and can fail while the test button still responds. Most manufacturers rate their units for five to seven years. Ohio homeowners in Cleveland, Cincinnati, and Dayton should check the manufacture date on the back of every detector and replace anything older than the rated lifespan.

Prevention: The Annual Furnace Inspection

Nothing prevents carbon monoxide problems as effectively as an annual professional inspection. A licensed technician will inspect your furnace for safety issues before the heating season begins. The visit should include:

  • Inspecting the heat exchanger for cracks, corrosion, and warping
  • Cleaning and adjusting the burners for proper combustion
  • Testing combustion efficiency with calibrated analyzers
  • Verifying the flue and chimney draft correctly and are free of blockages
  • Checking carbon monoxide levels around the furnace and in the living space
  • Confirming the blower, thermostat, and safety controls operate properly

Schedule this inspection in September or early October. Once the first cold snap hits, HVAC technicians across Columbus, Cleveland, Cincinnati, Toledo, and Akron are booked solid, and wait times stretch to weeks. Early fall service means you are not competing for appointments when you actually need heat.

Other Household Sources of Carbon Monoxide

Your furnace is not the only risk. Carbon monoxide can come from a gas water heater, a gas range used for long periods without ventilation, a wood-burning fireplace with a blocked chimney, a portable generator run too close to the house, or a vehicle left idling in an attached garage.

During winter power outages, never run a generator indoors or in an attached garage, and keep it well away from windows and vents. Clear snow and debris away from exterior furnace and water heater vents, which can become blocked during Ohio snowstorms and force exhaust back into the home. Never use a gas oven or range to heat a home.

What to Do If Your CO Alarm Sounds

If an alarm sounds and anyone feels symptoms, evacuate immediately and call 911 from outside. If no one feels ill, open doors and windows, shut off fuel-burning appliances if you can do so safely, and leave the building while you call for help.

Do not treat a sounding alarm as a false positive. Do not press the silence button and go back to sleep. Call your gas utility or a licensed HVAC technician to investigate. If a technician finds a genuine problem, they will identify the source, repair or replace the faulty component, and verify that carbon monoxide levels return to zero before restoring service.

Building CO Safety Into Your Fall Routine

Carbon monoxide safety works best as a short seasonal ritual. Every September, before you rely on your furnace, work through this sequence: replace furnace filters, test and if needed replace every CO detector, clear exterior vents of debris, and schedule a professional furnace inspection. When the technician completes the visit, ask for the combustion analysis results so you have a baseline for future years.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my furnace is leaking carbon monoxide?

You generally cannot see or smell it. Watch for a yellow burner flame, soot around the furnace, moisture on nearby windows, or rust on the flue. A working CO detector and an annual professional inspection are the only reliable ways to confirm a leak. If an alarm sounds, leave the home.

Is a carbon monoxide detector required in Ohio homes?

Ohio requires carbon monoxide alarms in residential buildings that contain fuel-burning appliances or attached garages. If you have a gas furnace, gas water heater, or fireplace, install detectors on every level and outside each sleeping area.

How much does a furnace safety inspection cost in Ohio?

A professional furnace tune-up and safety inspection in Columbus, Cleveland, or Cincinnati typically runs 100 to 200 dollars. It covers heat exchanger inspection, combustion testing, burner cleaning, and flue verification. It is the best-value safety step of the year.

Should I shut off my furnace if my CO alarm goes off?

Leave the building first. If no one feels ill and you can safely reach the furnace, shut it off at the switch or breaker, then call for help. Do not delay evacuation to shut anything down, and do not restart the furnace until a technician has inspected it.

Do new furnaces produce carbon monoxide?

All gas furnaces produce a small amount of carbon monoxide as part of combustion. A properly installed and maintained furnace vents it safely outside, keeping indoor levels near zero. The risk comes from incomplete combustion or a failure in the heat exchanger or flue, not the age of the unit alone.

How often should carbon monoxide detectors be replaced?

Replace CO detectors every five to seven years, or sooner if the manufacturer specifies a shorter lifespan. The sensor degrades silently, so a unit that passes its test button can still fail to detect a real leak. Check the date on the back of each detector this fall.
