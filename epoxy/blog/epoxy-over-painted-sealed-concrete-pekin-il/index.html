Can You Epoxy Over Painted or Sealed Concrete in Peoria IL?

Published September 2026 9 min read Serving Peoria, Pekin & Central Illinois

It is the first question almost every caller asks before booking an estimate in Peoria IL: "My garage floor is already painted, or it has that shiny sealer on it. Can you just coat over the top?"

The honest answer is sometimes yes, and often no. Whether a new epoxy system can go over an existing coating depends on what is on the slab, how well it is bonded, and whether it can be profiled. This guide walks through how to tell the difference before you spend money on a floor that peels off in a year.

Why This Matters More in Central Illinois

Garage and basement slabs in Peoria, Pekin, East Peoria, and the surrounding towns have usually been treated with something over the decades. Older homes near the river in Pekin often have a rubberized or acrylic "concrete paint" rolled on in the 1990s. Newer builds in Morton and Washington may have a clear curing sealer left by the concrete contractor.

Both layers sit between your new coating and the actual concrete. Epoxy bonds to clean, opened, mechanically profiled concrete. It does not bond through a barrier, and it does not bond to old paint at all. Anything on top of that surface is a potential failure layer, and in Illinois the failure usually shows up as sheet delamination after the first hard winter. For the wider comparison of painted floors versus a proper coating, see epoxy versus painted concrete in Peoria.

The Three Types of Existing Surface You Will Find

Before any decision is made, the surface has to be identified. There are three common situations, and they behave differently.

1. Clear Curing Sealer

Contractors sometimes apply a clear film-forming sealer to new concrete to slow moisture loss during curing. It looks glossy and repels water. Permanent sealers must be removed before coating. Dissipating resin sealers wear off over a couple of years, but still need verification.

2. Paint or Acrylic "Epoxy Paint"

This is the most common and the most problematic. Big-box epoxy paint is usually a water-based acrylic, not a true two-part epoxy. It sits on the surface as a film. Coating over it is a gamble: if the paint is already peeling anywhere, the new floor will follow it off. Even well-bonded paint is a weak link because the epoxy bonds to paint, not concrete.

3. An Existing, Well-Bonded Coating

Occasionally you find a sound, professionally installed coating that is fully adhered with no lifting. This is the one case where a recoat can make sense, but only after an adhesion test and proper abrasion of the existing surface.

The Water Test: A 60-Second Check You Can Do Yourself

Pour a small puddle on the slab in a few spots, including where tires park. If it soaks in and darkens the concrete within a few minutes, the surface is open and likely coatable. If it beads up and sits there like water on a waxed car, the slab is sealed or painted and needs mechanical removal before any coating will hold. A beading puddle is the fastest indicator that a "coat right over it" quote is going to be a problem.

Why Grinding Is Not Optional

The correct way to prepare a coated or sealed slab is diamond grinding. A grinder with diamond tooling removes the existing film and simultaneously opens the pores of the concrete underneath, creating the mechanical profile epoxy needs to lock into. This is the difference between a floor that lasts fifteen years and one that fails in a single season. Acid etching, which most consumer kits recommend, cannot remove paint or sealer and cannot create a proper profile on a sealed slab. For the full sequence from grinding through priming, see the epoxy flooring preparation process.

What the Failure Looks Like When Prep Is Skipped

When a coating is applied over paint or sealer without removal, failure is predictable: sheet delamination (the coating lifts in large sheets rather than wearing thin, because it is separating from the paint layer, not the concrete), blistering from trapped moisture, edge lifting along control joints and the garage apron, and rapid winter failure as freeze-thaw widens any weak bond. If this has already happened, the fix is removal and rebuild, not another coat on top. Our guide to epoxy floor repair in Peoria explains that process.

The Adhesion Test for an Existing Coating

If you have a sound existing coating and want to know whether a recoat is realistic, the professional move is an adhesion test before committing.

A small area is abraded, cleaned, and a test patch of the new system is applied. Once cured, the patch is scored into a grid and pulled with tape or a pull-off gauge. If it lifts cleanly, the bond is good enough to proceed. If it comes up with the patch, the substrate needs full removal. Any contractor willing to quote a recoat without running this test is guessing with your money.

Questions to Ask a Contractor About Your Existing Floor

Bring these to any estimate: Will you be diamond grinding, or coating over what is there? How did you determine what is currently on the slab? If there is paint or sealer, is removal included? Will you run an adhesion test before the full install? What is the topcoat, and is it UV stable? Clear, specific answers are a good sign. Vague ones, or a claim that grinding is unnecessary in this climate, should send you looking elsewhere.
